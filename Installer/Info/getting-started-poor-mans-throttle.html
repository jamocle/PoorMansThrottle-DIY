How PMT can be installed

Choose the power arrangement that fits your railroad

Poor Man's Throttle can be used in several different power configurations. The detailed guide below walks through the major arrangements so you can see where PMT sits in the power path and decide which approach best matches your locomotive and operating style.

1

PMT between transformer and track

PMT is installed between the transformer and the track, allowing the throttle system to control power before it reaches the rails.

2

PMT installed in the locomotive with track power

PMT rides onboard the locomotive while the locomotive still receives its primary power from the rails.

3

Deadrail operation with onboard battery only

The locomotive operates from its own onboard battery, eliminating the need to depend on track power for propulsion.

4

Transformer and battery hybrid

A hybrid arrangement combines transformer/track power with onboard battery capability for a more flexible installation.

See the complete PMT power-configuration guide View the diagrams and full explanation of each installation method before deciding how you want to wire your system.

Choosing battery power

Batteries for model railroading

Battery selection becomes much easier when you separate two ideas: voltage determines the electrical pressure available to the locomotive, while mAh describes how much energy capacity you are carrying. The right pack is the one that fits your locomotive, motor driver, electronics, and physical space — not simply the pack with the biggest numbers.

V

Voltage = power headroom

Higher battery voltage gives the motor driver more voltage to work with and can increase available top speed. Too much voltage can damage the motor or electronics, so always stay within the ratings of the locomotive, motor driver, buck converter, and accessories.

mAh

mAh = fuel-tank size

A 2,000 mAh pack is a 2.0 Ah battery. More mAh generally means longer runtime, but also a physically larger and heavier battery. Sound, lighting, smoke, automation, and heavy trains all increase current draw.

Wh

Wh = best energy comparison

Watt-hours account for both voltage and capacity: Wh = volts × amp-hours. This is the best way to compare the total stored energy of packs that use different voltages.

Quick runtime example: a 2,000 mAh battery is 2.0 Ah. If a locomotive averages about 0.5 A while running, the simple theoretical estimate is 2.0 ÷ 0.5 = 4 hours. Real-world runtime will be lower because current draw changes with load, acceleration, grades, sound, lights, and the amount of battery capacity you choose to use before recharging.
Practical starting points

Battery recommendations by scale

These are starting-point recommendations, not hard limits. Individual locomotives can vary substantially. Measure the locomotive's current draw and confirm its motor voltage before choosing a pack. The recommendations assume common lithium-ion / LiPo-style packs, where a cell is roughly 3.6–3.7 V nominal and about 4.2 V when fully charged.

Scale Good starting voltage Useful capacity range Why / notes
G Scale 4S–5S Li-ion / LiPo
14.8–18.5 V nominal · 16.8–21.0 V fully charged 		5,000–10,000 mAh Large-scale locomotives usually have the most room for batteries and often draw the most current. Start around 4S for moderate-voltage locomotives; use 5S only when the motor and the complete PMT power path are rated for it.
O Scale 4S Li-ion / LiPo
14.8 V nominal · 16.8 V fully charged 		3,000–6,000 mAh A strong general starting point for larger O-scale locomotives. Smaller locomotives or lower-voltage motors may be happier on 3S.
S Scale 3S–4S Li-ion / LiPo
11.1–14.8 V nominal · 12.6–16.8 V fully charged 		2,000–4,000 mAh 3S is a conservative starting point. Move to 4S only when the locomotive motor and driver are comfortable with the higher full-charge voltage.
HO Scale 3S Li-ion / LiPo
11.1 V nominal · 12.6 V fully charged 		1,500–3,000 mAh A practical match for many HO installations while keeping battery size manageable. Capacity is often limited by available internal space.
N Scale 2S Li-ion / LiPo
7.4 V nominal · 8.4 V fully charged 		500–1,500 mAh Space and weight dominate N-scale battery choices. 2S is the safest general starting point. A 3S pack can provide more voltage, but only use it when the motor, driver, and available space make it appropriate.
Battery safety matters more than scale. Use a protected pack or appropriate BMS for the battery chemistry and cell count, use the correct charger, and add a fuse close to the battery. Never choose a pack based only on nominal voltage — check the fully charged voltage too. An INA219 is useful for monitoring, but battery monitoring should not be treated as the only protection against cell over-discharge.

Reference context: the NMRA's DCC electrical standard uses typical power-station values around 12 V for N, 15 V for HO/S/O, and 18 V for large scales. PMT is not a DCC command station, so those values are useful context rather than direct battery-pack prescriptions. Common lithium-ion cells are typically about 3.6–3.7 V nominal and charge to about 4.2 V per cell. NMRA S-9.1 · Battery University: Lithium-ion voltages
